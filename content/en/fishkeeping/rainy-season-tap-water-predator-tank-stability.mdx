Your water-change routine worked all dry season. Same bucket, same conditioner dose, same schedule. Then the rains arrive, and the fish sulk after a change that used to be uneventful.

Nothing in your tank changed. The water coming out of your tap did.

Heavy rain changes the raw water behind the tap. Treatment plants adjust for it. What reaches your bucket in November is not quite the water you tested in August. This article is a seasonal routine for that shift: test the incoming water, change less at a time, and stop trusting a routine the season has already changed.

## What heavy rain does to tap water

Rain does not fall into your tank. It falls on the catchment, the river, or the reservoir your water comes from. After heavy rain, that raw water carries far more turbidity: silt, mud, and organic matter stirred up by runoff.

Treatment plants respond by adjusting. Coagulant dosing goes up to clear the turbidity, and operators watch pH and alkalinity because the chemistry of the incoming water has shifted. Chlorine dosing is adjusted too, because organic matter consumes chlorine before it can disinfect. Residual chlorine at your tap can therefore vary more after storms than it does in a calm dry spell.

None of this means your tap water is unsafe for you. It means it is more variable. For a 300-liter predator tank, variability is the problem. Arowana and other large predators handle a stable, slightly imperfect parameter far better than a parameter that swings.

The season makes this timely. BMKG expects the 2026/2027 rainy season to start across most of Indonesia in November to December 2026. It arrives later than usual under a very strong El Niño and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole, with the peak running December 2026 to February 2027. The transition is now. The routine below is for the months ahead, not for one storm.

## Test the tap, not just the tank

Most keepers test the tank. In rainy season, test the incoming water first. Three checks, one bucket, before the water goes anywhere near the fish.

First, temperature. Match the new water to the tank within 2°C. Arowana are generally kept between 24°C and 30°C, and the exact number matters less than stability. Sudden temperature drops are more harmful than a slightly low but steady temperature. A bucket of rain-cooled tap water poured into a warm tank is a temperature shock wearing a water change as a disguise.

Second, pH. Compare the tap water with the tank water. A sudden pH shift stresses fish even when both readings sit inside the acceptable range. If the tap reads far from the tank, slow the change down: smaller volume, or age the water first.

Third, chlorine and chloramine. Use a conditioner that explicitly treats chloramine, not just chlorine. Chloramine is chlorine bonded to ammonia, and breaking that bond leaves ammonium behind, which a total-ammonia test kit will read. That mechanism is covered in detail in this site's chloramine-trap article. The rainy-season version of the lesson is simpler. After heavy rain or nearby pipe work, the disinfectant load in your tap water can be higher than usual, so test the conditioned bucket once instead of assuming the usual dose handled it.

If the conditioned bucket reads ammonia on a total-ammonia kit, that is your tap water contributing it, not your filter failing. Expect the tank reading to reflect it after the change, and judge the situation by free ammonia and fish behavior, not by the total number alone.

## Change less, more often, and know when to wait

The dry-season habit of a large weekly change is the riskiest version of this routine in rainy season. A large change imports a large volume of variable water at once. Smaller, more frequent changes import the same total volume with less swing each time.

A practical transition rule: halve the volume per change and double the frequency for the first month of the rains. Watch the fish and the readings for two weeks before deciding whether your tap water is stable enough to return to the old schedule.

There are also days to skip. Right after an extreme downpour, or when the tap runs cloudy or smells more strongly of chlorine than usual, postpone a non-urgent change by a day. The tank will not crash in 24 hours from a skipped change. It can crash from a large change of bad water.

Two rules from the rest of this site still apply, and rainy season is the worst time to break them. Never rinse filter media under tap water, and never replace all media at once. A stressed fish in variable water needs its biofilter intact more than ever.

## Feed to the weather

Cooler, darker rainy days slow a predator's metabolism. The fish moves less, eats less, and digests more slowly. Overfeeding in those conditions is one of the biggest rainy-season mistakes: uneaten food decays, ammonia rises, and the biofilter works harder in exactly the conditions where the incoming water is least predictable.

Feed slightly less, and make sure everything is eaten quickly. If the fish ignores food it usually takes, skip the next meal rather than adding more. Appetite returning is a better signal than the calendar.

## The checklist

Before the change:

- Test the tap water: temperature, pH, and chlorine or chloramine.
- Compare temperature with the tank. Within 2°C, or warm the bucket first.
- Dose conditioner for the volume you are changing, exactly as the label directs. If you add water directly to the tank, many labels direct dosing for the full tank volume instead. Follow your product's label.
- If the tap is cloudy or smells strongly of chlorine after a storm, postpone a non-urgent change.

During the change:

- Change a smaller volume than your dry-season habit.
- Add the new water slowly, not in one pour.
- Keep the heater running and check it still holds temperature afterward. Night drops during heavy rain are when a weak heater shows itself.

After the change:

- Watch the fish for an hour: gasping, clamped fins, or frantic swimming mean act, regardless of test numbers.
- Retest the tank the next morning: ammonia, nitrite, and pH.
- If fish are gasping or acting drunk, treat it as an emergency: increase surface agitation, stop feeding for 24 to 48 hours, and do a change with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water.

## Conclusion

Rainy season does not demand new equipment. It demands a different order of operations: test the water before it enters the tank, change less of it at a time, and let the weather set the feeding schedule. The keeper who tests the tap in November is the keeper whose fish never notice the season changed.

Stability is the whole game. Test the incoming water, and the tank takes care of itself.
