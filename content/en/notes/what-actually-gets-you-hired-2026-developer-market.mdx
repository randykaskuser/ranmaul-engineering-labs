Two numbers describe the 2026 developer market, and they cannot both be true. Except they are.

As of 15 October, layoffs.fyi counted 131,937 tech workers laid off in 2026, already past the whole of 2025, which totalled 122,606. And US software-development postings are up nearly 15% since late February 2025, according to Indeed Hiring Lab data reported by CNBC. Both are true at the same time. Which one you feel depends mostly on how senior you look on paper.

## The numbers that explain the market

**131,937 tech layoffs in 2026, more than all of 2025.** That is layoffs.fyi's live total as of 15 October, across 321 companies. The year still had eleven weeks to run.

**Software postings up nearly 15% since late February 2025.** Indeed Hiring Lab's data, reported by CNBC on 11 October, shows US software-development postings rising while overall postings fell 7% over the same stretch.

**Still 27.5% below pre-pandemic levels.** The rebound follows a steep retreat from the 2022 hiring peak. This is not a return to the old market.

**71% of the posting increase went to senior roles.** Between May 2025 and May 2026, senior roles accounted for 71% of the increase in software-development postings, per Indeed. Note the wording: 71% of the increase, not 71% of all vacancies.

**37% of the increase carried AI in the title.** Over the same May-to-May window, job titles mentioning AI accounted for 37% of the increase. The two categories overlap heavily.

**Employment of developers aged 22 to 25 fell 20%.** That is Stanford's 2026 AI Index, reported by CNBC. Across AI-exposed fields, employment for that age group fell 16% since generative AI arrived, while older developer roles expanded.

**One in three pull requests now involves an AI agent.** GitHub reported in October that one in three pull requests involves an AI agent, up from fewer than one in ten a year earlier.

## What the numbers actually mean

This is not a market recovering. It is a market recomposing. Companies are hiring back at the top of the ladder while the bottom rungs stay sawn off.

The mechanism is not mysterious. When an AI agent can produce a plausible first draft of almost any routine task, the scarce skill changes. It stops being "can write code" and becomes "can tell whether the code is right, and what breaks if it is not." That is a senior skill by definition. It is also why the rebound concentrates in senior and AI-titled roles while junior employment shrinks.

The longer-term forecast is not bleak. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects software-developer employment growing 10% from 2025 to 2035, from about 1.72 million to 1.89 million jobs. That is a projection for the profession, not a promise of easier entry for today's graduates.

## The wrong answer: more tutorials

The instinct when the market tightens is to collect more credentials: another course, another certificate, another tutorial series. It feels productive. It is mostly invisible to a hiring manager.

A tutorial completion proves you can follow instructions. The market in 2026 is drowning in people who can follow instructions, because an AI agent can follow them faster. What a recruiter cannot get from a model is evidence that you made a decision, lived with it, and fixed it when it was wrong.

Indeed economist Sneha Puri put it plainly to CNBC: companies still need developers to oversee automated coding, refine products, and help clients integrate technology into daily operations. The demand is for people who direct AI, not people who compete with it on output volume.

## What actually gets you hired

Two kinds of proof, both verifiable in ten minutes by a stranger.

**One system you owned end to end.** Not a tutorial clone. Something you shipped, with real constraints: users, or at least real data, real trade-offs, and real failure modes. The proof is not the repository. It is the record of decisions inside it: why this architecture, what you rejected, what broke in production, and what you changed after.

**Demonstrated AI oversight.** GitHub's own warning makes this concrete: more AI-generated code brings more security exposures, and developers must prevent them or reduce the human effort needed to fix them. If your work shows you catching an AI agent's mistake — a wrong assumption, a subtle bug, a security hole — that is worth more than ten projects the agent wrote cleanly.

Concretely, a hiring manager should be able to open one link and find a running project. Its README should explain the decisions and the trade-offs. And it should include one write-up of something that went wrong and how you diagnosed it. If those three things are missing, no number of certificates fills the gap.

## Checklist before you apply

- One shipped project with real constraints, documented decisions, and a failure write-up.
- A public record of reviewing AI output: a caught bug, a corrected design, a security fix.
- A resume that names outcomes, not tools. "Cut p95 latency from 900ms to 210ms" beats "experienced with Redis."
- A short list of companies whose stack and problems you actually understand, not a hundred generic applications.
- One person who has seen your work and will say so by name.

## Conclusion

The 2026 market rewards proof, not preparation. Layoffs are up, postings are up, and both facts point the same way: employers are paying for people who can own a system and check what the machine writes. Build the evidence, make it checkable, and the split market works in your favour.
